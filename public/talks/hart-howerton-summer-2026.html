The Problem

The Fire Nobody Planned For

There's a counterintuitive lesson from forest management that changed how we think about wildfires — and it turns out to apply almost perfectly to Revit models. We spent two months testing the math to find out where the analogy holds, and where it breaks down.

Beat 1 of 3 · The Forest

For decades, Yellowstone looked perfectly fine

Through most of the 20th century, the U.S. Forest Service suppressed every wildfire it could. The forests looked dense and green. No smoke, no flames. From the outside, everything appeared healthy.

What nobody could see: the forest floor was filling up with dead wood, dry grass, and decades of accumulated fuel.

Beat 2 of 3 · The Accumulation

Suppressing small fires made the big ones inevitable

Small fires are nature's way of clearing fuel. They're uncomfortable and messy — but they prevent catastrophe by consuming the material before it can accumulate into something unmanageable.

When you suppress them year after year, the fuel doesn't disappear. It just builds, invisibly, until the conditions are right for something much worse.

Beat 3 of 3 · The Cost

When fire finally came, nothing could stop it

In the summer of 1988, Yellowstone burned. Not because one fire started — but because decades of suppression had turned the entire forest into fuel. 793,000 acres. The largest fire in the park's recorded history.

The lesson the ecologists took away: you cannot prevent risk by ignoring it. You have to manage it in small, controlled doses.

Beat 1 of 3 · The Model

On any given Monday, the model opens and syncs normally

Nobody flags anything urgent. The project moves forward. Deadlines are met. From the outside — and from inside Revit — everything looks fine.

But underneath, the model's floor is filling up too. In-place families that should've been loaded components. CAD imports from an early schematic that nobody cleaned. Design options left open from three milestones ago.

Beat 2 of 3 · The Drift

The warnings don't announce themselves — they just accumulate

Each sprint adds a little more. The model gets heavier. Syncs slow down. Someone turns off a workset that was tracking something important and forgets to turn it back on.

None of these are individually alarming. Collectively, they're fuel. And nobody's measuring the fuel level.

Beat 3 of 3 · The Crisis

By the time it shows up, months of drift is now a week of crisis

A coordination review where nothing aligns. A sync failure. A model too bloated to open cleanly on the client's workstation. A week before a deadline.

The model didn't fail all at once. It drifted. Quietly. And nobody had a dashboard to see the score drop in time to do anything about it. Until now.

The Maths

Where the Analogy Holds — and Where It Breaks

We tested three mathematical hypotheses drawn from the wildfire literature against 351 k sync records. Two were clean nulls. One held up beautifully.

HOLDS

Memoryless accumulation

Error risk is flat once you condition on activity. Errors-per-sync is stationary — a project's history doesn't predict its next error beyond the rate you can measure right now.

P(error | sync, history) ≈ P(error | sync)
NULL

Self-excitation (errors beget errors)

A Hawkes process model finds apparent burstiness, but it entirely disappears once you control for sync activity — errors don't trigger more errors, busy periods trigger both.

Hawkes branching ratio: 0.87× (activity-controlled) ≈ 1 → no contagion
NULL

Critical slowing down before collapse

In forest fires, systems near a tipping point show rising autocorrelation and variance. We found neither — pre-surge AR(1) actually fell (−0.04 vs control +0.14). Collapses are stochastic, not critical.

Pre-surge AR(1): −0.04 · surrogate p = 0.54 (ns)

Finding 1

One giant team

226 of 227 modellers are connected through shared project work — one enormous collaboration network with no isolated cliques. Good news for training: a signal that reaches one person tends to reach everyone.

Finding 2

No modeller signature

Low-rank tensor decomposition of the user × project × check error cube shows the interaction term is near-zero. Errors reflect the project's condition — its age, complexity, and workload — not an individual's personal habits.

Finding 3

Broadcast beats blame

Since errors are driven by project state rather than individual style, the highest-leverage intervention is a firm-wide tip about the currently-failing check — not a private nudge to the user who happened to be active when health dropped.

"Here's where the wildfire analogy breaks down — and it's the most useful thing we found."

What We Found

Four Things We Didn't Expect

When you have 350,000 data points, you can test assumptions you've always held. Some of them held up. Others didn't. The ones that didn't are the most useful.

Finding 1 · Part A

We assumed time was the enemy

Makes sense intuitively: the older a model, the more chances for things to go wrong. The longer since someone checked it, the more drift you'd expect to find.

We tested it. The correlation between time elapsed and error count is essentially zero.

ρ = 0.02
Time vs. errors · near zero

Finding 1 · Part B

A model that sits untouched stays healthy

Time alone doesn't make models worse. A model that nobody is actively working on doesn't accumulate new errors. It just sits there, frozen in whatever state it was in.

This changes the question. Instead of "how old is the model?" the right question is "how active is the model?"

Finding 1 · Part C

Activity is what drives errors — and the chance to fix them

Sync count is the real predictor. Correlation of 0.67 — that's meaningful. The more work happening, the more errors appear. But also: the more saves, the more opportunities to improve.

The right metric is errors per sync, not errors per day. Measure the rate of work, not the passage of time.

ρ = 0.67
Sync count vs. errors · strong
Live example — in the dashboard

Project A is currently on the forecast watchlist. Health score 54, 6 saves in the last data window — errors track the busy periods, not the calendar. You can see its history in the Risk Monitor tab.

Finding 2 · Part A

We assumed there would be "clean" and "messy" modelers

Before we started, this was the working hypothesis: some people are careful and systematic; others are less so. Two types, maybe three. Simple categories that you could act on.

We ran four clustering algorithms on 197 modelers and 40 checks. K-means, Gaussian mixture, hierarchical, DBSCAN.

Finding 2 · Part B

There are no natural groups — just a continuous gradient

Silhouette score: 0.17. That's the number that says "yes, these are real groups." A score below 0.25 means weak or overlapping structure. 0.17 is essentially random.

The data is a gradient, not categories. Everyone is somewhere on a spectrum, and where you sit shifts with project type, phase, and deadline pressure.

0.17
Silhouette score — very weak clustering

Finding 2 · Part C

This changes what good training looks like

If there are no types, you can't train "the messy ones." But you can identify the specific checks that each person struggles with most — and target training there.

Person A's top issue might be in-place families. Person B's might be unplaced rooms. Same firm, same team, completely different training priorities. The system produces a per-person training list.

Finding 3 · Part A

Models don't just drift — they have turning points

When you plot health over time, you don't see a smooth decline. You see periods of relative stability, punctuated by moments where the trajectory definitively shifts.

These aren't noise. Using a Bayesian detection algorithm called BOCPD, validated by a statistical significance test, we can find the exact month where a project changed course.

Finding 3 · Part B

82 confirmed turning points across the firm

72 were deteriorations. 10 were recoveries. Each one statistically confirmed — not just a bad week, but a genuine regime change where the project's health trajectory shifted to a new level.

The recoveries are the interesting ones. When a project reversed course, what happened? Often: a cleanup sprint, a new person taking ownership, a deadline that forced a thorough review.

82

Confirmed regime shift — from the data

Project B: health dropped from 61 → 41 — a statistically confirmed 20-point deterioration. Projects C and D each show similar confirmed breaks. These aren't bad weeks; they're new baselines.

Finding 3 · Part C

"When did this go wrong?" now has an answer

For every project on the watchlist, you can now ask: when exactly did this start to decline? The system returns a date. And the data around that date — who was working, which checks started failing — gives you the story.

This moves the conversation from "the model is in bad shape" to "here's when it changed and here's what was happening." That's an actionable conversation.

Finding 4 · Part A

We mapped the collaboration network across the firm

Every time two people work on the same model — any Revit file, any project — they're connected. We traced every one of those connections across three years and all 14 active projects.

The question: is Hart Howerton a collection of small, siloed teams? Or is there more cross-project connection than anyone realized?

Finding 4 · Part B

The studios work as one firm, not three silos

Color the same network by studio and the picture is unmistakable: San Francisco, New York, and Palm Beach aren't separate islands. People move between projects across studios, stitching the whole firm into a single web of shared work.

Very few people work in isolation. Almost everyone shares projects with colleagues in other studios — which is exactly why the network holds together as one connected community instead of fragmenting into local cliques.

Finding 4 · Part C

When the firm learns something, it travels fast

This is an asset. A training tip that lands well on one project carries to the next through shared team members. Good habits propagate through the same network that currently propagates drift.

It also means the coaching loop doesn't need to reach everyone individually. The network does some of the work. You seed it in a few places; it spreads.

    What We Built

    A Health Score for Every Model, Updated Every Save

    Every Synch event, automatically checked. Every project, tracked over time. And a system that can tell you which projects are heading in the wrong direction before they get there.

    The System · Part 1

    40 checks on every single Synch event

    Not a manual audit. Not a monthly review. Every time someone saves to the central model, the system automatically runs the 40 quality checks we saw earlier — sorted into three severity categories: Red, Yellow, and Green.

    This is the "controlled burn" equivalent: small, frequent checks that catch issues while they're still easy to fix, rather than discovering them at a review.

    40
    Checks per sync · automatic

    The System · Part 2

    Each check has a severity — RED, YELLOW, or GREEN

    Not all problems are equal. An in-place family is a Red issue — it hurts performance and coordination. An unused view filter is Green — good to clean up, but not urgent.

    The severity determines the penalty weight: Red is 1.0, Yellow is 0.5, Green is 0.1. Grey means the check wasn't run — no penalty, no assumption.

    The System · Part 3

    One score from 0 to 100 — like a design review grade

    All the penalties add up. The score is 100 × (1 – total penalty ÷ maximum possible). A perfect model scores 100. The tiers:

    Critical · below 40 Warning · 40–70 Healthy · above 70

    Think of it like a drawing set review: instead of marking up 40 individual sheets, you get one number that tells you the overall condition — and which issues are pulling the score down.

    The System · Part 4

    The firm median sits in the Warning range — with room to improve

    Today, the firm's median project health score is in the Warning tier. That's honest — not a crisis, but not optimal either. The good news: we now know exactly which checks are driving the most penalty firm-wide.

    The top two failing checks firm-wide? Errors & warnings (nearly universal) and drafting elements placed directly on views instead of on sheets. Both are teachable and both have clear fixes.

    The Engineering

    A Pipeline That Runs While You Sleep

    Every Friday at 8 am, Azure pulls fresh data from BIMbeats and Guardian, scores all 449 projects, ranks the risks, and drafts the week's training tip — before anyone in the office opens a laptop.

    Layer 1 · Data

    Live from two sources

    BIMbeats streams every Revit sync event — element counts, warnings, save times. Guardian adds compliance checks. The ETL joins them, runs 40 rule checks, and writes a scored flat file to Azure Blob every week without anyone touching it.

    Layer 2 · Forecast

    Which projects are trending down?

    A TabICL foundation model trained walk-forward on 871 project-months identifies which currently-healthy projects are statistically likely to surge into a higher-risk band next month — 4.5× better than a persistence baseline at PR-AUC 0.245.

    Layer 3 · Coach

    The tip closes the loop

    Every Friday the pipeline picks the highest-failing check firm-wide, grounds a tip in the BIM Manual and Autodesk Help, and drafts an email the Design Technology lead approves before broadcast — detect, rank, tip, measure, repeat.

    The Dashboard · Level 1

    The firm's health, every month, in one line

    The Health tab opens on the firm's score over time — median health, the spread (the shaded band), and the 40 and 70 threshold lines so you can see at a glance where the firm sits and where it's trending.

    Three views: health over time, tier-mix over time (what fraction of projects are Critical / Warning / Healthy), and a score distribution. Click any month on the line to go deeper.

    The Dashboard · Level 2

    Click a month — see every active project and its score

    Every project active that month appears in a sorted list, color-coded by tier. You can see at a glance which projects were healthy, which were in warning, and which were critical — and this is the state at that specific point in time, not today.

    This is forensics mode: "let's go back to October and see what the model looked like when the coordination review happened."

    The Dashboard · Level 3

    Click a project — see who was working on it and how

    The third level shows every contributor who synced to that project in that month, their sync count, their average health contribution, and their error rate compared to the firm average.

    The framing matters: this is "who was active during this period" — not "who is responsible." The system shows activity, not blame. You decide what to do with it.

    Forecasting · Part 1

    The backward view is useful — the forward view is better

    Knowing a project's health score history tells you where it's been. The forecasting layer asks whether we can predict which projects will deteriorate significantly in the coming month.

    This is a hard problem. Project collapses are rare — only about 5% of project-months see a meaningful drop. But even a weak signal, if reliable, is actionable.

    Forecasting · Part 2

    Seven models tested. Five of seven beat random.

    From a simple logistic regression to a 2025 state-of-the-art tabular AI model. All tested with walk-forward validation — trained only on past data, predicting a future month, repeated across 763 project-months.

    The two time-series AI models failed — BIM health doesn't behave like weather or stock prices. The tabular models, which treat each project-month as a snapshot, worked much better.

    Forecasting · Part 3

    The best model is 4.5× better than guessing

    TabICL — a 2025 tabular foundation model — achieved a lift of 4.5× over random. ROC score of 0.857. Trained on 40 surge events across 108 projects.

    Honest caveat: at the top of the list, about 1 in 3 flagged projects historically deteriorated. This is a prioritisation list, not a guarantee. Use it to focus attention — not to alarm anyone.

    The Coaching Loop

    Data That Doesn't Change Behavior Is Just a Filing Cabinet

    The goal was never just to measure. It was to close the loop: detect problems, decide who needs to know, deliver the right message to the right person, and track whether anything actually changed as a result.

    Coaching Loop · Layer 1

    Tip of the Week — anonymous, firm-wide, grounded in the Manual

    Every Friday, the system identifies the highest-failing check firm-wide that hasn't been covered in the last nine weeks (a rotation to prevent repetition). It drafts an email grounded in a verbatim quote from the firm's BIM Manual — or Autodesk's official documentation where the manual has a gap.

    Two tips per email — one Modeling, one Drawing — both linking to LinkedIn Learning and Autodesk Help. No individual names. No accusation. Just: here's what the firm is struggling with and here's how to get better.

    Coaching Loop · Layer 2

    DT Lead monthly digest — the human-in-the-loop layer

    On the first Friday of each month, the DT Lead receives a private digest: which projects are on the watchlist, which checks are failing firm-wide, and which modelers have above-average error rates relative to how busy they are.

    The framing is coaching, not performance management. "Modeler with above-average rate" — worth a supportive conversation, not a review. The system routes harder situations to a human; it doesn't escalate automatically.

    Coaching Loop · Layer 3

    We measure whether the tips actually work

    Using difference-in-differences — a classic causal inference technique — we compare how the failure rate of a tipped check changes relative to untipped checks used as a control.

    If a tip works, the tipped item's failure rate drops relative to the control. The harness is built and waiting. The current status: "pending post-tip data" — which is expected, and the right way to do it. Instrument before you send.

    DRAWING
    Hidden elements in views
    shows up on 94% of models firm-wide — you're not alone

    Manually hidden elements are invisible landmines — they hide errors and confuse anyone who reuses the view.

    Quick fix

    1. Use Reveal Hidden Elements (light-bulb) to audit.

    Methods

    Health score — H = 100 × (1 – Σpenalty / Σmax_weight). Weights: RED 1.0, YELLOW 0.5, GREEN 0.1, GREY not scored. Tiers: Critical <40, Warning 40–70, Healthy ≥70.

    Regime detection — Bayesian Online Change Point Detection (BOCPD, NIG prior) + Welch t-test + Bonferroni correction. 82 confirmed points (72 degrading, 10 recovering).

    Forecasting — walk-forward validation, 763 project-months, 40 surge events (5.24% base rate). Winner: TabICL tabular foundation model (arXiv 2025), PR-AUC 0.245, lift 4.5×.
